Good Data Starts Before the Dashboard

A beautiful dashboard cannot repair an inconsistent definition, a missing record, or a duplicate transaction. Reliable reporting starts with decisions made long before the first chart.

By Nathan (Nate) Brenner · November 10, 2026 · Expanded November 11, 2026

A dashboard can make a dataset easier to understand. It cannot repair an inconsistent definition, a missing record, or a duplicate entry. Reliable reporting starts with the decisions made before the first chart.

That is particularly important when a report combines data from multiple systems, reporting periods, and business owners. The chart may be the most visible part of the work, but confidence depends on everything behind it: agreed definitions, tested transformations, reconciled totals, documented exceptions, and clear ownership.

1. Start with the meaning of a row

Write down what each record represents: a property, an account, a transaction, a fund, a document, or a reporting period. In analytics, this is often called the grain of the data. Mixing different levels of detail is an easy way to double-count values without an obvious error message.

Suppose one table contains a row per property per month, while another contains a row per transaction. Joining them directly on property ID could multiply monthly totals once for every matching transaction. Both source tables might be correct. The combined dataset would still be misleading.

Before joining or aggregating anything, define the expected grain, join keys, one-to-many relationships, and treatment of missing matches. If the final dashboard is meant to show annual totals by client and fund, make sure the transformation from source transactions to those annual figures is explicit and reviewable.

2. Make the essentials explicit

Identify fields that must be present, the values allowed, and the combinations expected to be unique. Keep these rules close to the dataset so the next delivery can be checked the same way.

Completeness

Are required client IDs, reporting periods, dates, and amounts present?

Validity

Do dates, codes, and financial values follow their defined formats and rules?

Uniqueness

Are apparent duplicates legitimate versions, or unintended repeated records?

Consistency

Do definitions, naming conventions, and calculations match across systems?

Rules should also explain exceptions. A null amount may be permitted for a pending record but unacceptable for a completed report. Two documents may share a client and year because one is an amendment. Good validation distinguishes legitimate business variation from preventable error.

3. Reconcile at the boundaries

Compare record counts and appropriate totals before and after a transformation. When totals change, make the reason visible. A documented exclusion is easier to explain than an unexplained difference discovered during a presentation.

For financial data, reconciliation should be planned rather than improvised. Start with an expected population: how many source files, records, entities, or funds belong in the period? Then define which totals should tie, where rounding is permitted, and what exclusions are acceptable.

A straightforward reconciliation log might contain the input row count, accepted row count, rejected row count, source amount, loaded amount, variance, and the reason for each approved adjustment. An unexplained variance should remain an exception—not become a silent plug.

Example reconciliation rule

Source total − documented exclusions + documented adjustments = approved loaded total. The formula is simple; the discipline is retaining evidence for each component.

4. Check the transformation, not just the final number

Two incorrect adjustments can cancel each other out and produce a total that looks right. That is why validating only a grand total is insufficient.

Inspect representative records through the full path: source field, mapping rule, transformed field, target table, and final report. Test unusual but plausible conditions, such as negative values, year-over-year changes, missing source fields, duplicate submissions, and revised records.

For a quarterly-to-annual conversion, establish whether the quarterly values are additive, point-in-time balances, or cumulative year-to-date figures. Summing four quarters makes sense for some measures and seriously distorts others. The business definition should determine the calculation.

5. Use SQL as a flashlight

Read-only SQL queries can help identify patterns that spreadsheets or dashboard summaries hide. For example, checking possible duplicates by client, period, and document type:

SELECT client_id, reporting_period, document_type,
       COUNT(*) AS row_count
FROM ReportingStage
GROUP BY client_id, reporting_period, document_type
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

This query flags candidates for investigation, not automatic deletion. Whether the rows are duplicates depends on the agreed grain, source IDs, revision policy, and business context.

Other practical queries look for null required fields, orphaned foreign keys, negative values where not allowed, dates outside the reporting window, and mismatches between staging and final tables. Turn repeatable findings into documented validation checks rather than repeatedly discovering them by accident.

6. Leave a useful trail

Record the source, reporting period, processing date, transformation version, and unresolved exceptions. The goal is a repeatable process that lets another person understand where a number came from and what still needs attention.

Data lineage does not require a complicated enterprise platform to be useful. A consistent batch identifier, source file name, load timestamp, mapping version, and exception summary can make an investigation dramatically more efficient.

For sensitive financial information, auditability and access controls belong in the same conversation. Keep evidence without exposing protected data in screenshots, unrestricted logs, or public examples.

7. Give each exception an owner

“Data needs review” is not a resolution. A useful exception record states which rule failed, what is affected, who will investigate, whether downstream work is blocked, and how the issue will be closed. If a client is waiting on the result, the status should also support an accurate update.

Sometimes the correct response is to fix a source file. Sometimes it is to update a mapping definition, revise a validation rule, or obtain business approval for an unusual condition. The resolution should be recorded so the same question does not have to be answered repeatedly.

8. Publish definitions alongside the dashboard

Before anyone uses a metric to make a decision, define its name, calculation, filters, currency, units, date boundaries, refresh schedule, and owner. “Total revenue” or “completed documents” may mean different things to different stakeholders unless those definitions are explicit.

A brief data dictionary or methodology page is often more valuable than another visualization. If a metric changes, communicate whether the business changed, the data changed, or the definition changed.

9. Measure data quality over time

Quality should improve as repeated problems are understood. Track missing-field rates, duplicate candidates, failed loads, unreconciled amounts, reopened exceptions, and time to resolution. Then ask which root causes contribute most to rework.

Not every number needs a dashboard of its own. The point is to have enough evidence to know whether the process is becoming more reliable.

The takeaway

Trustworthy reporting starts before the dashboard. Define the grain, validate the source, test the transformation, reconcile the totals, preserve lineage, and assign exceptions clearly. When those foundations are sound, dashboards become what they should be: an easier way to see a reality you already understand.

That approach takes a little more work upfront, but it can save hours of uncertainty when a client, colleague, or reviewer asks, “Where did this number come from?”
