The File Loaded. Is the Data Right?

A four-quarter reporting check shows how a file can pass validation and still give the wrong annual return.

By Nathan Brenner · November 21, 2026

Four quarterly records went in. Four came out. Every field passed validation. The annual report says an investor broke even. So why is their $100 investment worth $99?

If I were walking a new analyst through a reporting check, that’s where I’d start. You can solve this fictional example with a pencil, and doing it by hand tells you something a row count can’t.

What did the fund pay out?

Say a fund pays investors $1,000 in cash distributions each quarter. Someone reading the annual report wants to know how much cash the fund paid out during the year. Add the four payments and you get $4,000. A sum answers the question exactly.

What happened to $100?

Now move one column over. The fund reports a total return of 10% in the first quarter, a loss of 10% in the second, and zero in each of the last two. Apply the same sum and the annual report shows 0%. That’s a believable number. It fits the field, falls within a reasonable range, and looks perfectly ordinary beside the $4,000.

Here’s the check I’d do before signing off. Start with a hypothetical $100 invested at the beginning of the year and assume distributions are reinvested. The first quarter’s gain takes it to $110. The next quarter’s loss is 10% of $110, so the investor loses $11. With two flat quarters after that, the investment ends the year at $99. The annual total return is −1%.

Keep the example as a test

Give the reporting process those four quarterly records and write down the two annual figures you expect before you run it. If a future change produces $4,000 and 0%, an analyst can point to the precise problem and explain why it matters.

When I review a report, I like to turn a column heading into a question someone would actually ask. “How much cash did the fund pay out?” leads me to add the payments. “What happened to $100 invested for the full year?” leads me to follow the money through each quarter. Once I can answer the question on paper, I know what to ask the software to prove.
